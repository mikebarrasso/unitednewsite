<p><strong>Planning for a financially secure retirement usually means coordinating several pieces at once: income sources, investment risk, taxes, and regular reviews. This guide walks through five planning steps, with tables you can use as a starting point. No strategy can guarantee an outcome, and each choice involves trade-offs.</strong></p>

<h2>What "Secure Retirement" Means, and What It Does Not</h2>

<p>People use "secure retirement" to describe different things: having reliable income, being able to cover healthcare, not outliving savings, or simply feeling prepared. A plan can improve the odds of meeting those goals, but it cannot remove uncertainty. Markets, inflation, tax law, and health all change, which is why a plan works best as something you review and adjust.</p>

<p>The phrase also overlaps with the SECURE Act and SECURE 2.0, federal laws that changed retirement account rules, including required minimum distribution (RMD) ages and inherited account rules. It is also used for state "Secure Choice" savings programs for workers without an employer plan. If you are looking for how those laws affect beneficiaries, see our guide to the <a href="/blog/inherited-ira-10-year-rule">inherited IRA 10-year rule</a>. This article focuses on planning your own retirement.</p>

<h2>Step 2: Align Investment Risk With Your Timeline</h2>

<p>As retirement approaches, the question often shifts from "how much can this grow?" to "how will this fund the next several years, and what happens if markets fall?" Some retirees reduce stock exposure to lower volatility. Others keep a meaningful growth allocation because retirement can last decades and inflation erodes purchasing power. Both approaches carry risk: a lower stock allocation may not keep pace with inflation, and a higher one can decline sharply at the wrong time.</p>

<p>A common way to frame the decision is to match near-term spending with more stable assets and long-term spending with growth assets. Our guides to <a href="/blog/retirement-portfolio-strategies-market-downturns">retirement portfolio strategies for market downturns</a> and <a href="/blog/market-volatility-financial-plan-advisor">sequence of returns risk</a> go deeper on this. Our <a href="/services/investment-management">investment management</a> service is designed to keep the portfolio aligned with the plan.</p>

<h2>Step 4: Plan for Taxes Across the Years</h2>

<p>Taxes do not end at retirement, and the tax picture often changes year to year. Areas worth reviewing with your CPA and planner together:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Which accounts to draw from.</strong> Taxable, tax-deferred, and Roth withdrawals are taxed differently, and the best order can change over time.</li>
<li><strong>Roth conversions.</strong> Converting in lower-income years may reduce future required distributions, but a conversion is taxable when it happens. See our <a href="/blog/roth-conversion-strategy-pre-retirees">Roth conversion window guide</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Required minimum distributions.</strong> RMDs generally begin at age 73 or 75, depending on your birth year. Our guide to <a href="/blog/pre-retirement-tax-transition-managing-brackets-rmds">managing brackets before RMDs</a> explains how to plan ahead.</li>
<li><strong>Losses and gains in taxable accounts.</strong> Realized losses may offset gains, subject to limits and the wash-sale rule. See <a href="/blog/direct-indexing-tax-loss-harvesting">direct indexing and tax-loss harvesting</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Medicare premiums.</strong> Higher income can raise Medicare premiums two years later, so income spikes are worth planning around.</li>
</ul>

<p>Our <a href="/services/tax-planning">tax planning</a> team can review these together. None of these steps guarantees a lower tax bill, and results vary by situation.</p>

<h2>Step 5: Review and Adjust Every Year</h2>

<p>Retirement is not a "set it and forget it" plan. Markets change, goals evolve, and factors such as healthcare costs, tax law, and family needs shift over time. A yearly review is a good rhythm:</p>

<ul>
<li>Compare actual spending with the plan</li>
<li>Confirm your cash reserve and where the next year of withdrawals will come from</li>
<li>Review the asset mix against your risk tolerance and timeline</li>
<li>Estimate this year's taxable income and any planned conversions</li>
<li>Confirm RMD amounts once they apply</li>
<li>Update beneficiaries and estate documents after any major life change</li>
</ul>
