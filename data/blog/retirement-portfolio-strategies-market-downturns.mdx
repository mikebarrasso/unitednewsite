<p><strong>A market downturn matters most in the years around retirement, because withdrawals can compound a decline. This guide covers how to review portfolio risk, size a cash reserve, choose a withdrawal approach, and coordinate taxes, so the plan can flex when markets fall. Results vary and every approach has trade-offs.</strong></p>

<h2>Why Timing Matters More Near Retirement</h2>

<p>A decline early in retirement can have a different effect than the same decline years earlier. While you are working, a lower market can be an opportunity to keep contributing. Once you are drawing income, withdrawals come from a smaller balance, which leaves fewer assets in place to participate in a later recovery. This is often called sequence of returns risk.</p>

<p>The risk is about the order of returns as much as their average, and it is one reason a downturn plan is usually built before markets fall, not during. Our guide to <a href="/blog/market-volatility-financial-plan-advisor">sequence of returns risk in retirement</a> covers the mechanics in more depth.</p>

<h2>Step 1: Review Your Portfolio's Current Exposure</h2>

<p>Start with what you own and how you plan to use it. These questions can help frame a review:</p>

<ul>
<li>How many years of spending could be covered without selling growth assets?</li>
<li>How much of the portfolio is sensitive to interest rate changes?</li>
<li>Does your income plan depend on markets performing in a particular way?</li>
<li>Is any single holding, employer stock position, or sector a large share of the total?</li>
<li>Which accounts are taxable, tax-deferred, or Roth, and how would a withdrawal from each be taxed?</li>
<li>How would you respond, in practice, to a large decline in your statement balance?</li>
</ul>

<p>The last question matters more than it may seem. A portfolio you can stay invested in tends to be more useful than one that looks better on paper but is hard to hold through a difficult stretch.</p>

<h2>Step 4: Coordinate Taxes With the Downturn Plan</h2>

<p>Taxes affect which assets you sell and when. A few areas worth reviewing with your CPA and planner together:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Tax-loss harvesting.</strong> In a taxable account, realized losses may offset realized gains, subject to annual limits and the wash-sale rule. It does not apply to IRAs or 401(k) accounts, and the tax benefit depends on your situation. See our overview of <a href="/services/tax-planning">tax planning</a> and our guide to <a href="/blog/direct-indexing-tax-loss-harvesting">direct indexing and tax-loss harvesting</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Roth conversion timing.</strong> A lower account balance may mean a conversion moves more shares for the same taxable amount. A conversion is taxable in the year it occurs, and it should be weighed against your bracket, Medicare premiums, and cash needs. Our <a href="/blog/roth-conversion-strategy-pre-retirees">Roth conversion window guide</a> covers the trade-offs.</li>
<li><strong>Which account to draw from.</strong> Pulling from taxable, tax-deferred, or Roth accounts produces different tax results, and the order can change over time.</li>
<li><strong>Required minimum distributions.</strong> RMDs can force withdrawals during a decline. Our guide to <a href="/blog/pre-retirement-tax-transition-managing-brackets-rmds">managing brackets between career income and RMDs</a> explains how to plan ahead for them.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Step 5: Write Down Your Rules Before You Need Them</h2>

<p>Many households find it easier to stay on plan when the decisions were made in advance and in writing. A short downturn policy can include:</p>

<ul>
<li>Where the next year of spending will come from if markets fall</li>
<li>What triggers a rebalance, and who makes the call</li>
<li>Which discretionary expenses can flex, and by roughly how much</li>
<li>When you will review the plan with your advisor and CPA, such as a decline of a set size or a scheduled annual meeting</li>
</ul>

<p>A written process does not remove uncertainty. It can make responses to it more deliberate.</p>

<h2>A Simple Annual Downturn Checklist</h2>

<ul>
<li>Confirm the size and location of your cash reserve</li>
<li>Review the stock and bond mix against your plan</li>
<li>Check for concentrated positions, including employer stock</li>
<li>Estimate this year's taxable income and any planned conversions</li>
<li>Confirm RMD amounts and which account will fund them</li>
<li>Update your withdrawal plan for changes in spending, health, or income</li>
</ul>
