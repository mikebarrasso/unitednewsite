<p><strong>Major goals such as retirement, college, and a home purchase have different timelines, so they usually call for different accounts and different levels of investment risk. This guide shows how to sort goals by time horizon, compare account types, and coordinate the tax side. Results vary and every approach has trade-offs.</strong></p>

<h2>Start by Sorting Goals by Time Horizon</h2>

<p>Before choosing investments, list each goal with the date you expect to need the money and how much flexibility you have. The same dollar can be invested very differently depending on when it is needed. A goal ten years away can usually tolerate more ups and downs than a goal two years away, because there is more time to recover from a decline. Time is not the only factor. How firm the date is, and what happens if the money falls short, matter too.</p>

<h2>Retirement: Two Time Horizons, Not One</h2>

<p>Retirement has a saving phase and a spending phase, and both can last decades. That means money set aside for retirement is not all needed on the day you stop working. Some of it may stay invested for twenty or thirty more years. Questions worth working through:</p>

<ul>
<li>How much of your income are you saving, and are you capturing any employer match?</li>
<li>Are contributions going to pre-tax, Roth, or taxable accounts, and why?</li>
<li>How might inflation and a longer life expectancy affect the income you need?</li>
<li>If you are within 5 to 10 years of retirement, how will withdrawals, Social Security, and taxes fit together?</li>
</ul>

<p>The choice between pre-tax and Roth contributions is a tax trade-off, not a right or wrong answer, and it can change as your income changes. For the broader picture, see our <a href="/services/retirement-planning">retirement planning</a> services.</p>

<h2>College Funding: Rules Matter as Much as Returns</h2>

<p>College costs have often risen faster than general inflation, which is why many families start early. The account you use affects taxes, control, and financial aid. Common options include state-sponsored 529 plans, custodial accounts, and regular taxable accounts. Each has different rules:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Qualified expenses.</strong> Education accounts generally favor spending on qualified costs. Withdrawals for other purposes may be taxed and may carry additional charges.</li>
<li><strong>State rules.</strong> Tax treatment and plan features vary by state, so check the rules that apply to you.</li>
<li><strong>Financial aid.</strong> Where the money sits can change how it is counted on aid forms. This is worth reviewing before you fund an account, not after.</li>
<li><strong>Changing investment mix.</strong> Many plans shift toward more stable holdings as the enrollment date approaches. A fixed mix may no longer fit the timeline.</li>
</ul>

<p>College funding also competes with retirement saving for the same dollars. A plan can compare both goals together, so neither is funded at the expense of the other without a conscious decision.</p>

<h2>Shorter-Term Goals: Protect the Date</h2>

<p>For a home purchase or other large expense within a few years, the date often matters more than the return. A market decline shortly before the purchase leaves little time to recover. Many households keep this money in stable, liquid holdings, accepting that growth may be modest and may not keep pace with inflation. If the timeline is flexible, you may be able to take somewhat more risk, but that is a decision to make on purpose.</p>

<h2>Equity Compensation Adds a Tax Timeline</h2>

<p>If part of your pay comes as RSUs, stock options, or an employee stock purchase plan, a goal can be funded or delayed by events you do not fully control, such as a vest date, an exercise window, or a liquidity event. Each of these can create taxable income in a single year. Setting aside cash for the tax bill is a separate goal from investing the shares, and it is easier to plan before the event than after. Our guide to <a href="/blog/equity-compensation-planning-startup-employees">equity compensation planning for startup employees</a> covers the mechanics, and our <a href="/services/equity-compensation">equity compensation</a> page describes how we coordinate the tax side.</p>

<h2>Where Taxes Enter the Decision</h2>

<p>Two people with the same goal can reach different after-tax results depending on which account holds which assets. A few areas to review together:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Account type.</strong> Taxable, tax-deferred, and Roth accounts are taxed differently on contributions, growth, and withdrawals.</li>
<li><strong>Asset location.</strong> Placing investments in the account type that suits their tax treatment may help reduce taxes, though results depend on your situation and rules can change.</li>
<li><strong>Withdrawal order.</strong> The order in which you draw from accounts can affect your tax bracket and Medicare premiums in retirement.</li>
<li><strong>Realized gains and losses.</strong> Selling to rebalance or fund a goal can create a taxable event in a taxable account.</li>
</ul>

<h2>An Annual Goals Checklist</h2>

<ul>
<li>List every goal with a target date and a rough cost</li>
<li>Mark each goal as firm, flexible, or optional</li>
<li>Confirm which account is earmarked for each goal</li>
<li>Check whether each investment mix still fits the time remaining</li>
<li>Review upcoming vest, exercise, or bonus dates and the tax they may create</li>
<li>Confirm retirement contributions and any employer match</li>
<li>Review beneficiary designations and education account rules</li>
<li>Schedule a review with your planner and CPA together</li>
</ul>
