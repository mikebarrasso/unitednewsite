<p><strong>Tax-deferred accounts postpone tax on eligible contributions or investment earnings until money is withdrawn, rather than eliminating tax altogether. Traditional retirement accounts commonly use this treatment. For someone nearing retirement, the timing of withdrawals can affect taxable income and other decisions, so the account balance is only one part of the plan.</strong></p>

<h2>What Does Tax-Deferred Mean?</h2>
<p>Tax deferral means an applicable tax is paid later, not necessarily avoided. In a traditional IRA, contributions may be deductible depending on eligibility, while earnings generally are not taxed until distribution. The taxable portion of a withdrawal is generally included in income; any nondeductible basis can change that result. See the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/traditional-and-roth-iras">IRS comparison of traditional and Roth IRAs</a> and <a href="https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b">IRS Publication 590-B</a> (reviewed October 13, 2026).</p>
<p>Common tax-deferred retirement account categories include traditional IRAs and pre-tax portions of employer plans such as 401(k)s and 403(b)s. An account name alone does not tell you the tax treatment of every dollar: after-tax contributions and designated Roth portions can follow different rules. “Tax-deferred investment account” is often shorthand for a retirement account's tax treatment, not a promise that every investment held there is tax-free.</p>

<h2>Taxable, Tax-Deferred, and Roth Accounts: What Changes?</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Taxable accounts:</strong> There is generally no retirement-account deferral. Interest and dividends may be taxable as received, and selling an investment may create a taxable gain or deductible loss. The result depends on the type of income, basis, and holding period. See IRS guidance on <a href="https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc403">interest</a>, <a href="https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc404">dividends</a>, and <a href="https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409">capital gains and losses</a> (reviewed October 13, 2026).</li>
<li><strong>Tax-deferred traditional accounts:</strong> Eligible contributions may lower current taxable income; earnings generally accumulate without annual income tax inside the account. Taxable distributions generally count as income when taken. A deduction is not automatic, and nondeductible amounts require separate accounting. See the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/traditional-and-roth-iras">IRS IRA comparison</a> (reviewed October 13, 2026).</li>
<li><strong>Roth accounts:</strong> Contributions generally do not receive an upfront deduction. Qualified distributions generally are not taxable, but nonqualified distributions may be partly taxable. Rules vary by account and distribution. See the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/traditional-and-roth-iras">IRS IRA comparison</a> and <a href="https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b">Publication 590-B</a> (reviewed October 13, 2026).</li>
</ul>

<p>No treatment is preferable for everyone. A choice that changes taxes today may create a different tax burden later, and liquidity needs, plan rules, and individual circumstances matter.</p>

<h2>How Do Withdrawals and Required Distributions Fit Together?</h2>
<p>A withdrawal from a traditional tax-deferred account can add to taxable income in the year it is taken, apart from any previously taxed basis. Taking money before applicable eligibility rules are met may also result in additional tax unless an exception applies. Withdrawal timing should therefore be considered alongside spending needs and other income. The <a href="https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b">IRS distribution guide</a> explains the IRA rules (reviewed October 13, 2026).</p>
<p>Required minimum distributions (RMDs) limit how long certain retirement accounts can remain untouched. The starting age depends on birth year. As of October 13, 2026, IRS final regulations published in <a href="https://www.irs.gov/irb/2024-33_IRB">Internal Revenue Bulletin 2024-33</a> set the applicable age at 73 for people born from 1951 through 1958 and at 75 for people born in 1960 or later; the IRS has proposed age 73 for those born in 1959. Many pre-retirees today fall in the age-75 group, so the age quoted in older articles, and on some IRS summary pages that still read age 73, may not match your birth year. The <a href="https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plan-and-ira-required-minimum-distributions-faqs">IRS RMD FAQs</a> explain that the owners of traditional IRAs, SEP IRAs, and SIMPLE IRAs must begin once they reach the applicable age, even if retired, while a workplace-plan participant may be able to delay the start until retirement unless they are a 5% owner. Roth IRAs and designated Roth accounts generally do not have lifetime RMDs for the original owner, though beneficiaries have separate rules. The first IRA RMD can generally be delayed until April 1 of the following year, which may put two distributions in that calendar year. Confirm the rule that applies to each account and birth year before making a withdrawal decision.</p>
<p>Income coordination extends beyond the income-tax return. The <a href="https://www.irs.gov/faqs/social-security-income">IRS explains</a> that whether Social Security benefits are taxable depends in part on other income. The <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/OP_Home/handbook/handbook.25/handbook-2501.html">Social Security Administration explains</a> that income-related Medicare premium adjustments use a measure of income generally drawn from an earlier tax return. A taxable retirement withdrawal could affect these calculations if it changes the relevant income enough; no particular outcome is assured. Sources reviewed October 13, 2026.</p>

<h2>A Short Decision Framework for Pre-Retirees</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Map each account's tax treatment.</strong> Note traditional, Roth, and taxable balances, including whether a traditional IRA has nondeductible contributions. Review account and tax records rather than guessing from the label.</li>
<li><strong>Map the years.</strong> Compare expected work income, retirement spending, Social Security timing, and applicable RMD dates. Consider how withdrawals could interact with taxes and Medicare premiums; assumptions may change.</li>
<li><strong>Test options together.</strong> Ask which accounts may supply cash when it is needed, what taxes a distribution may create, and whether keeping flexibility matters more than seeking a lower current tax bill. Revisit the plan as rules and circumstances change.</li>
</ol>
