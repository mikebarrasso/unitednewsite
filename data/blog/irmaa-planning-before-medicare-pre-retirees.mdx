<p><strong>IRMAA is an income-related surcharge on Medicare Part B and Part D premiums. Social Security generally sets it using your tax return from two years earlier, so income at age 63 can affect premiums at 65. Planning before enrollment means understanding which income counts and what trade-offs each choice carries.</strong></p>

<h2>What Is IRMAA and How Does the Two-Year Lookback Work?</h2>
<p>IRMAA stands for income-related monthly adjustment amount. It is an extra amount added to the standard Medicare Part B premium and to Medicare prescription drug (Part D) coverage when your income is above certain levels. The <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/medicare/medicare-premiums.html">Social Security Administration</a> explains that it generally uses the most recent federal tax return the IRS provides. For 2026 premiums, that is generally the return for tax year 2024 (SSA, reviewed November 2, 2026).</p>
<p>The measure of income is modified adjusted gross income (MAGI), which SSA defines as adjusted gross income plus tax-exempt interest. Because of the two-year lookback, a tax decision made in the year you turn 63 may show up in your Medicare premiums when you turn 65. That is why IRMAA belongs in the planning conversation before Medicare begins, not after the first premium letter arrives.</p>

<h2>Which Income Counts Toward IRMAA?</h2>
<p>Anything that raises adjusted gross income can raise MAGI. For pre-retirees, the common items are taxable withdrawals from traditional IRAs and 401(k)s, Roth conversions, interest and dividends, realized capital gains, and tax-exempt interest, which SSA adds back. Qualified Roth distributions are generally not included in gross income, according to the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/traditional-and-roth-iras">IRS comparison of traditional and Roth IRAs</a>. The tax treatment of each item depends on the account and the facts, so a CPA review of the return matters.</p>
<p>This is where coordinated planning helps. A decision that looks sensible for income tax alone, such as a larger Roth conversion in a single year, can also change the Medicare premium two years later. Looking at both sides together is the kind of work our CFP&reg; and CPA team does side by side.</p>

<h2>What If Your Income Drops After You Retire?</h2>
<p>Because Social Security looks back two years, a premium can be based on income from a year when you were still working. SSA explains that it may make a new decision if your income has gone down because of certain life-changing events, including marriage, divorce, the death of a spouse, work stoppage or reduced hours, or loss of income-producing property. You request this using <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/forms/ssa-44.pdf">Form SSA-44</a>, and SSA asks for documentation of the event and the reduction in income (<a href="https://www.ssa.gov/medicare/lower-irmaa">SSA request page</a>, reviewed November 2, 2026). SSA decides each request on its own facts, and a request does not guarantee a reduction.</p>

<h2>An Annual IRMAA Planning Checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Early in the year:</strong> Review last year's return for MAGI, and note how close it came to a bracket line.</li>
<li><strong>Before age 63:</strong> Map expected income for the two years that will set your first Medicare premiums, including any Roth conversions, sales, or equity events.</li>
<li><strong>Midyear:</strong> Check realized gains and withdrawals against the plan so a surprise does not arrive in December.</li>
<li><strong>Year-end:</strong> Confirm any charitable distributions, conversions, and gain harvesting with your CPA before the year closes.</li>
<li><strong>After retirement or a life change:</strong> Compare your current income with the year Medicare is using, and ask whether an SSA-44 request applies.</li>
<li><strong>Each fall:</strong> Review the new CMS figures and your own Social Security premium notice.</li>
</ol>
