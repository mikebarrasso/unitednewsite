<p><strong>Many non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited IRA within 10 years of the owner's death, and annual distributions may also be required depending on the owner's status and account type. A surviving spouse generally has additional options, such as treating the IRA as their own. Review the rules before deciding when to take a distribution.</strong></p>

<h2>What Is the Inherited IRA 10-Year Rule?</h2>

<p>The 10-year rule generally applies to a non-spouse designated beneficiary who inherited an IRA from an owner who died in 2020 or later and who is not an eligible designated beneficiary. Under the rule, the inherited IRA generally must be fully distributed by December 31 of the 10th calendar year following the year of death.</p>

<p>That deadline is not necessarily a recommendation to wait until year 10. Distributions from a traditional inherited IRA are generally included in taxable income. Taking all distributions in one year could concentrate income, while taking them too quickly could have different tax and cash-flow consequences. The appropriate pace depends on the beneficiary's income, deductions, other distributions, and broader planning circumstances.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b">IRS's Publication 590-B</a> provides the governing beneficiary distribution framework. The final RMD regulations generally apply to distribution calendar years beginning in 2025, following prior transition relief for certain missed beneficiary RMDs.</p>

<h2>Are Annual RMDs Required During Years One Through Nine?</h2>

<p>The answer often turns on whether the original owner died before or on or after their required beginning date (RBD). For many traditional IRA owners today, the RBD is April 1 following the calendar year in which they turn 73. An owner who dies after turning 73 but before that April 1 date is generally treated as having died before the RBD.</p>

<p>An annual RMD is a minimum distribution, not a schedule that automatically empties the account. A beneficiary may distribute more than the minimum, but the full account must still be distributed by the applicable deadline. Taking more in one year does not necessarily remove a later annual-RMD obligation.</p>

<p>A separate distribution may be due for the year of the owner's death. If the owner had reached the required beginning date and had not taken the full RMD for that year before death, the beneficiary may need to complete that distribution. The account's records and custodian guidance can help confirm what remains due.</p>

<h2>Who Is an Eligible Designated Beneficiary?</h2>

<p>Some beneficiaries are not subject to the standard 10-year framework in the same way. The IRS identifies several categories of eligible designated beneficiaries:</p>

<ul>
<li>A surviving spouse</li>
<li>The original owner's minor child, until the child reaches the applicable age of majority</li>
<li>A disabled individual</li>
<li>A chronically ill individual</li>
<li>An individual who is not more than 10 years younger than the original owner</li>
</ul>

<p>These classifications have technical definitions and can affect the distribution method and timing. A surviving spouse may have options that are not available to other beneficiaries, including treating an inherited IRA differently from a non-spouse beneficiary (see the <a href="#surviving-spouse">surviving spouse section</a> below). Trust provisions, multiple beneficiaries, and successor beneficiaries can also change the analysis. This is an area where the beneficiary designation and account paperwork matter as much as the account balance.</p>

<h2 id="surviving-spouse">How Do Inherited IRA Rules Differ for a Surviving Spouse?</h2>

<p><strong>A surviving spouse who is the sole beneficiary generally has more choices than other beneficiaries. The spouse may treat the IRA as their own, roll it into their own IRA, or remain a beneficiary of an inherited IRA. Which path fits depends on the spouse's age, the owner's required beginning date, and account details.</strong></p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-beneficiary">IRS explains</a> that a spouse has more options than a non-spouse beneficiary when the spouse is the sole beneficiary, and that sole-beneficiary status is determined by September 30 of the year after the year of the owner's death. A non-spouse beneficiary, by contrast, cannot treat an inherited IRA as their own, cannot make contributions to it, and generally cannot roll amounts into or out of it, although a trustee-to-trustee transfer to another inherited IRA may be possible.</p>

<p>For an inherited Roth IRA, a spouse who treats the account as their own is generally treated like the original owner, and <a href="https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b">Publication 590-B</a> notes that an original Roth IRA owner has no lifetime required distributions. That can matter for a spouse who does not expect to need the money soon, although liquidity needs and the five-year holding period still deserve a look.</p>

<p>Neither path is better in every case. The trade-offs often turn on the spouse's age relative to 59&frac12;, whether the spouse expects to need distributions soon, the owner's required beginning date, and whether other beneficiaries share the account. A spouse who is not the sole beneficiary, or who inherits through a trust, may have a different set of choices. This is a place where a coordinated CFP&reg; and CPA review can help, because the election affects both the tax return and the household's income plan. Review the beneficiary designation and custodian paperwork before making an election.</p>

<h2>Inherited Roth IRAs Follow a Different Pattern</h2>

<p>An inherited Roth IRA can still be subject to the 10-year deadline, but the original Roth IRA owner has no lifetime RBD. As a result, a typical non-eligible designated beneficiary subject to the 10-year rule generally does not have annual RMDs during years one through nine. The account generally must still be emptied by the end of year 10.</p>

<p>Inherited Roth IRA distributions are generally tax-free when the original owner's Roth IRA satisfied the five-year holding period. If that period had not been met, earnings may be taxable. The absence of annual RMDs does not make timing irrelevant. A beneficiary may still need to coordinate withdrawals with liquidity needs, estate administration, and other tax decisions.</p>

<h2 id="inherited-ira-taxes">How Are Inherited IRA Distributions Taxed?</h2>

<p><strong>Distributions from an inherited traditional IRA are generally taxed as ordinary income to the beneficiary, except for any portion that represents after-tax basis. Inherited Roth IRA distributions are generally tax-free when the five-year period has been met. Timing, other income, and the account's basis can change the result.</strong></p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-beneficiary">IRS states</a> that beneficiaries must include any taxable distributions they receive in gross income, and that IRA beneficiaries may take a lump-sum distribution of the inherited account at any time. A larger distribution in one year can raise taxable income for that year, which is one reason the pace of distributions deserves attention even when the rules allow flexibility.</p>

<p>Three other points can affect the tax picture:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Early-distribution tax.</strong> Distributions to a beneficiary after the owner's death are generally not subject to the 10% additional tax on early distributions, even when the beneficiary is under age 59&frac12;. A surviving spouse who treats the IRA as their own may lose that treatment.</li>
<li><strong>Estate tax deduction.</strong> A beneficiary may be able to deduct federal estate tax paid on certain income in respect of a decedent when reporting a traditional IRA distribution. Whether this applies depends on whether federal estate tax was paid on the account.</li>
<li><strong>Net investment income tax.</strong> IRA distributions generally are not net investment income, but they can count toward the modified adjusted gross income threshold. Our <a href="/blog/net-investment-income-tax-2026-year-end-guide">net investment income tax year-end guide</a> explains how that threshold works.</li>
</ul>

<p>State income tax treatment varies by state, so confirm the rules where the beneficiary lives. Tax results vary, and no distribution schedule should be assumed to produce a particular result.</p>

<h2>Five Planning Questions to Review Before Taking a Distribution</h2>

<h3>1. When did the original owner die?</h3>
<p>Deaths before 2020, deaths in 2020 or later, and successor-beneficiary situations may follow different rules. Start by confirming the date of death and whether this is the first inherited account or an account inherited from a prior beneficiary.</p>

<h3>2. Had the original owner reached the required beginning date?</h3>
<p>This determines whether annual RMDs may be required during the 10-year period. The answer is not always the owner's age alone, because the RBD is defined by a separate date rule.</p>

<h3>3. What kind of beneficiary is involved?</h3>
<p>A spouse, minor child, disabled or chronically ill beneficiary, or beneficiary close in age to the original owner may be treated differently. Do not assume the standard 10-year rule applies without first confirming the beneficiary category.</p>

<h3>4. Is the account traditional or Roth?</h3>
<p>Both account type and distribution timing can affect the tax discussion. A coordinated review can help clarify how inherited-account distributions fit alongside wages, retirement income, charitable giving, and other taxable events. Tax results vary, and a distribution should not be assumed to produce a particular result.</p>

<h3>5. Are there other deadlines or documents to review?</h3>
<p>Beneficiary forms, trust documents, the custodian's account records, prior-year distribution history, and estate-administration deadlines may all matter. Keeping these records together can reduce the risk of acting on an incomplete set of facts.</p>

<h2>How an Inherited IRA Fits Into a Coordinated Plan</h2>

<p>An inherited IRA is rarely an isolated tax question. Distribution timing may intersect with retirement-income planning, charitable intentions, a future Roth conversion analysis, or a household's expected income over several years. These decisions have trade-offs, and the applicable rules can be complex.</p>

<h2>Key Takeaway</h2>

<p>The inherited IRA 10-year rule creates a firm end-date for many beneficiaries, but it does not create a one-size-fits-all withdrawal schedule. Confirm the owner's date of death and RBD status, the beneficiary category, the account type, and the available documentation before deciding how distributions fit into the years ahead.</p>
