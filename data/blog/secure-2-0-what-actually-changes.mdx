<p>SECURE 2.0 is not one rule that changed on one date. It is a collection of retirement provisions arriving on different calendars. The useful question is not “What is in the law?” It is “Which decisions does this change for my household?”</p>

<p>Three areas deserve the most attention: when required minimum distributions begin, how catch-up contributions work for older employees, and when unused 529 savings can move to a beneficiary's Roth IRA.</p>

<h2>1. RMDs Begin Later—but the Extra Years Need a Job</h2>

<p>Required minimum distributions generally begin at age 73 for people reaching the current starting cohort, with age 75 applying to later cohorts under the statutory schedule. The first-year deadline can create two taxable distributions in one calendar year if the initial RMD is delayed until the following April, so “later” does not automatically mean “better.”</p>

<p>The opportunity is the gap between retirement and RMDs. Those lower-income years may be used for measured Roth conversions, realizing capital gains, or drawing from tax-deferred accounts before distributions become mandatory. The right move depends on future tax brackets, Medicare premiums, charitable plans, and cash needs.</p>

<h2>2. Catch-Up Contributions Now Have More Moving Parts</h2>

<p>Employees ages 60 through 63 may receive a higher catch-up limit in many workplace plans. Beginning in 2026, participants whose prior-year wages from the sponsoring employer exceed the indexed threshold generally make catch-up contributions on a Roth basis when the plan offers catch-ups. The dollar limits and wage threshold are indexed, so confirm the current amounts and the plan's implementation before changing payroll.</p>

<p>This is not simply a contribution-limit story. Roth catch-ups trade a current deduction for potentially tax-free qualified withdrawals. That can be sensible for a high earner expecting similar future tax rates, but it also changes current withholding and cash flow. The election belongs inside the broader tax projection.</p>

<h2>3. A 529-to-Roth Rollover Is a Release Valve, Not a Shortcut</h2>

<p>Unused 529 assets may be eligible for a direct trustee-to-trustee rollover into a Roth IRA for the 529 beneficiary. The account generally must have been open for at least 15 years; annual Roth IRA contribution limits apply; the lifetime rollover limit is $35,000; and recent contributions and related earnings are excluded under the five-year rule.</p>

<p>The provision can help a family that funded education conservatively and finished with money left over. It is not a way to bypass every Roth contribution rule, and changing a beneficiary or adding money shortly before the rollover can complicate eligibility. Coordinate with the 529 provider and Roth IRA custodian before moving funds.</p>

<h2>A Better Implementation Checklist</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Map the dates.</strong> Record the retirement date, Social Security target, Medicare enrollment, RMD starting age, and any year a participant turns 60 through 63.</li>
<li><strong>Run a multi-year tax projection.</strong> Compare planned Roth conversions and withdrawals across the low-income window rather than optimizing one year at a time.</li>
<li><strong>Check the employer plan.</strong> Confirm catch-up limits, Roth availability, payroll timing, and how prior-year wages are measured.</li>
<li><strong>Inventory old 529 accounts.</strong> Gather the opening date, contribution history, beneficiary, and remaining balance before assuming a rollover qualifies.</li>
<li><strong>Coordinate the professionals.</strong> The investment plan, tax return, payroll election, and Medicare projection should use the same assumptions.</li>
</ol>

<h2>What We Are Doing With Client Plans</h2>

<p>We are adding the new dates and thresholds to each retirement timeline, then testing the choices together: Roth conversions before RMDs, Medicare premium exposure, charitable distributions, workplace catch-ups, and any leftover education savings. SECURE 2.0 creates options, but the value comes from sequencing them around the household's actual plan.</p>
