<p><strong>Direct indexing holds individual securities rather than a single pooled fund, which may create more opportunities to realize losses in a taxable account. Whether the added cost and complexity make sense depends on tax circumstances, account structure, other realized gains, and how the approach fits within an overall financial and tax plan.</strong></p>

<h2>What Is Direct Indexing?</h2>

<p>Direct indexing is a way to seek broad market exposure by owning individual securities in a separately managed account rather than owning shares of a single pooled fund. Each position has its own cost basis and may have its own unrealized gain or loss.</p>

<p>That ownership structure can create planning flexibility in a taxable account. A pooled fund is generally sold or retained as one position. With direct indexing, individual positions can be reviewed separately, which may create opportunities to realize losses while maintaining a broadly diversified allocation through different holdings. The approach also introduces additional trading, operational complexity, and the possibility that results differ from the benchmark the account seeks to track.</p>

<p>The relevant question is not whether direct indexing is inherently better than a pooled-fund approach. It is whether the potential tax-planning flexibility is sufficient to justify the additional cost, complexity, and tracking difference for a particular household.</p>

<h2>How Does Tax-Loss Harvesting Work?</h2>

<p><strong>Tax-loss harvesting</strong> involves selling an investment that has declined in value to realize a capital loss. That loss may offset capital gains, and a net capital loss may offset up to $3,000 of ordinary income annually. Unused net capital losses may generally be carried forward to future tax years, subject to applicable tax rules.</p>

<h3>Fund-Level Harvesting</h3>

<p>When an investor owns a single fund position, the opportunity to realize a loss depends on the cost basis and value of that position as a whole. Even if some underlying companies have declined, the fund itself may have an unrealized gain. In that circumstance, selling the fund would generally realize a gain rather than a loss.</p>

<h3>Security-Level Harvesting</h3>

<p>A direct indexing account holds individual securities, each with a separate cost basis. When some holdings decline while others rise, a manager may identify losses in specific positions and consider selling them. Proceeds may be reinvested in other securities designed to maintain the intended market exposure, while avoiding a purchase that could trigger wash-sale treatment.</p>

<h2>A Hypothetical Planning Scenario</h2>

<p><em>This example is hypothetical and for illustration only. It does not represent a client account, recommendation, or expected outcome.</em></p>

<p>Consider an investor with a taxable account, a recently realized capital gain, and a separately managed account holding a broad group of individual securities. During the year, some positions may decline even if the account as a whole does not. A review may identify losses that could be realized and used against eligible gains, subject to wash-sale rules and the investor's full tax situation.</p>

<p>The result is not automatic. The amount of available loss, the character and timing of gains, the investor's tax circumstances, implementation costs, and market conditions all matter. In a year with limited price dispersion, there may be few or no losses to consider harvesting.</p>

<h2>The Wash-Sale Rule</h2>

<p>The wash-sale rule can prevent a taxpayer from recognizing a loss when substantially identical securities are purchased within 30 days before or after the sale that generated the loss. The disallowed loss is generally added to the basis of the replacement shares, which may defer rather than permanently eliminate the potential tax benefit. The timing window spans 61 days: the 30 days before the sale, the day of sale, and the 30 days after.</p>

<p>Wash-sale analysis may require coordination across taxable accounts, retirement accounts, and accounts held by a spouse. A purchase in another account can affect the intended tax treatment of a sale elsewhere. This is one reason tax-loss harvesting should be reviewed alongside the investor's complete account and tax picture, rather than handled as an isolated trade.</p>

<p>Direct indexing is not a default solution for every taxable investor. Its potential value depends on several factors that should be considered together.</p>

<p>For some households, a simpler approach may be more appropriate. For others, particularly those with taxable assets, realized gains, and a need for coordinated tax review, direct indexing may be worth evaluating. Neither result should be assumed without examining the costs, restrictions, and tax implications in context.</p>

<h2>How Direct Indexing Fits Into a Coordinated Tax Plan</h2>

<p>Tax-loss harvesting is one input in a broader planning process, not a standalone solution. Its relevance may change based on realized gains, charitable giving, equity compensation, business transactions, retirement income, and other tax decisions made during the year.</p>

<h3>Roth Conversion Planning</h3>

<p>Roth conversions create ordinary income, while capital losses generally offset capital gains first. For that reason, a harvested loss does not automatically create additional room for a Roth conversion. The limited ordinary-income offset and the interaction of gains, deductions, and other income can still matter in a broader tax projection. A coordinated review helps clarify whether these decisions affect one another in a particular year.</p>

<h3>Capital Gains and Tax Thresholds</h3>

<p>Realized capital losses may offset realized capital gains, which can affect the tax treatment of transactions completed during the year. The potential effect depends on the character of the gains and losses, their timing, and the taxpayer's broader income picture. It should be reviewed before assuming a loss will change a particular bracket or threshold.</p>

<h3>Charitable Giving and Concentrated Holdings</h3>

<p>Charitable giving and concentrated positions can introduce separate tax and planning considerations. For example, a proposed gift of appreciated property may have different tax treatment than a sale, and deduction limits and documentation rules may apply. For investors evaluating a concentrated holding, our <a href="/blog/tax-efficient-diversification-unwind-concentrated-stock">guide to tax-efficient diversification</a> discusses questions that may arise when planning a transition. Business owners and startup employees managing <a href="/blog/equity-compensation-planning-startup-employees">equity compensation</a> may face related coordination questions.</p>

<h2>2026 Capital Gains Tax Brackets</h2>

<p>The tax effect of a realized loss depends in part on the gain it offsets. Long-term capital gains on assets held for more than one year are generally taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20%, based on taxable income.</p>

<h2>Limits and Trade-Offs</h2>

<p><strong>Taxable accounts are the focus.</strong> Gains and losses inside many tax-advantaged accounts are not currently recognized for tax purposes, so tax-loss harvesting generally does not serve the same purpose there.</p>

<p><strong>Opportunity can vary over time.</strong> A newly funded account may have different loss-harvesting opportunities than an account with substantial embedded gains. Market conditions and price dispersion also change from year to year.</p>

<p><strong>Costs and tracking difference matter.</strong> Owning and trading many individual securities can involve management costs, administrative complexity, and results that differ from the benchmark the account seeks to follow. Any potential tax benefit should be weighed against those trade-offs.</p>

<p><strong>It does not resolve an unsuitable allocation.</strong> Tax-loss harvesting is a tax-management technique. It does not replace a review of goals, liquidity needs, time horizon, risk tolerance, or an oversized position in a single holding.</p>

<p><strong>Outcomes vary.</strong> There may be little or no loss to harvest during periods of limited price dispersion. No tax or investment outcome should be assumed based on a particular strategy or a prior market environment.</p>
