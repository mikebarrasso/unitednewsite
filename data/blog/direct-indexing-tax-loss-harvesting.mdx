<h2>What Is Direct Indexing?</h2>

<p>If you already own a broad-market index fund or ETF, you understand the basic idea: instead of trying to beat the market, you buy exposure to hundreds of companies at once, at low cost, and let compounding do the work over time. <strong>Direct indexing</strong> takes that same idea and changes one thing: instead of buying shares of a fund that holds those hundreds of companies, you buy the individual stocks in the index directly, held in a separately managed account titled in your own name.</p>

<p>The distinction sounds technical, but it has real consequences. When you own a fund, you own one asset: a share of the fund. When you own the underlying stocks directly through a direct indexing account, you own dozens or hundreds of separate positions, each with its own cost basis and its own gain or loss. That structural difference is what makes <strong>direct indexing and tax-loss harvesting</strong> a meaningful combination for taxable investors, and it is worth understanding clearly before deciding whether it belongs in your plan.</p>

<p>This is exactly where our review process matters. Every strategy discussed on this page is evaluated by our team of CFP&reg; professionals and CPAs working together, not by a financial plan built separately from your tax return. That coordination, not any single tactic on its own, tends to determine whether a strategy like this earns its added complexity for a given household.</p>

<p>Minimum investment requirements for direct indexing accounts have also declined meaningfully in recent years. Where minimums historically began near $250,000, providers across the industry now offer direct indexing accounts with minimums as low as $5,000, and commonly in the $25,000 to $100,000 range for professionally managed programs, based on 2025 and 2026 industry pricing. That accessibility is one reason the strategy has moved from a niche institutional tool toward a mainstream option for taxable investors with meaningful, though not necessarily enormous, portfolios.</p>

<h2>How Does Tax-Loss Harvesting Work?</h2>

<p><strong>Tax-loss harvesting</strong> is the practice of selling an investment that has declined in value to realize a capital loss, then using that loss to offset capital gains elsewhere, and potentially up to $3,000 of ordinary income each year, with any unused loss carried forward indefinitely to future tax years.</p>

<h3>Fund-Level Harvesting: What an Index Fund Can (and Cannot) Do</h3>

<p>When you own a single index fund or ETF, tax-loss harvesting is an all-or-nothing decision. The fund as a whole is either above or below your cost basis. If the overall market or index is up for the year, even modestly, there is usually no loss to harvest at all, even though some individual companies inside that index lost value during the same period. You are limited to harvesting at the level of the entire fund position, which means a loss is only available when the market itself has fallen enough to put your specific purchase underwater.</p>

<h3>Security-Level Harvesting: What Direct Indexing Adds</h3>

<p>Because a direct indexing account owns each underlying stock separately, losses can be harvested company by company, even in a year when the overall index is flat or positive. In any diversified group of 200 to 500 individual stocks, some will decline over a given period even while the index as a whole rises, simply because markets do not move in lockstep. A direct indexing account can systematically identify those individual laggards, sell them to realize the loss, and reinvest the proceeds into a similar, but not identical, security to maintain overall market exposure. This is sometimes described as harvesting &ldquo;tax alpha,&rdquo; though the size of that benefit depends heavily on market conditions, portfolio size, and how recently the account was funded.</p>

<p>In this hypothetical, systematically harvesting losses across the underlying positions over the course of the year could offset somewhere in the range of $15,000 to $30,000 of realized capital gains elsewhere in the portfolio, for example gains recognized when rebalancing a concentrated stock position or selling a legacy holding. The realistic range for any individual account depends on how recently it was funded (newly funded accounts typically have the most harvesting opportunity), overall market dispersion that year, and portfolio size. A single index fund position offers none of this granularity: it either has an unrealized loss as a whole, or it does not.</p>

<h2>The Wash-Sale Rule and How to Avoid It</h2>

<p>The IRS wash-sale rule exists specifically to prevent investors from claiming a tax loss while economically staying in the same position. Under the rule, if you sell a security at a loss and buy the same security, or one the IRS considers &ldquo;substantially identical,&rdquo; within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss is disallowed for tax purposes. Instead, the disallowed loss is added to the cost basis of the replacement shares, which defers the tax benefit rather than eliminating it outright. That 61-day window, 30 days before the sale, the day of the sale, and 30 days after, applies across all of your accounts, including IRAs and a spouse&rsquo;s accounts, not only the account where the sale occurred.</p>

<p>This is precisely where owning individual securities creates an advantage over a single fund position. When a direct indexing account harvests a loss on one company&rsquo;s stock, the proceeds are typically reinvested into a different company in a similar industry or with similar characteristics, rather than back into the identical stock. Because the replacement security is not substantially identical to the one sold, the wash-sale rule does not disallow the loss, and the account maintains similar overall market exposure while the tax loss is preserved. Replicating this same discipline manually, security by security, across hundreds of positions and multiple accounts, is difficult to do consistently without a systematic process, and a single mistake, such as a spouse buying the same stock in an unrelated account, can quietly disallow a loss you were counting on.</p>

It generally provides no benefit inside an IRA, 401(k), or other tax-advantaged account, since gains and losses inside those accounts are not currently taxable and therefore cannot be &ldquo;harvested&rdquo; for a tax benefit.</p>

<h2>How Direct Indexing Fits Into a Coordinated Tax Plan</h2>

<p>Tax-loss harvesting produces the most value when it is treated as one input into a broader tax plan, not a standalone tactic running in a separate account that your advisor and your tax preparer never discuss with each other.</p>

<h3>Roth Conversions</h3>

<p>Harvested losses reduce your recognized capital gains for the year, which frees up additional room in your current tax bracket. For pre-retirees actively managing a <a href="/blog/roth-conversion-strategy-pre-retirees">Roth conversion window</a>, that freed-up bracket space can sometimes be redirected toward a larger conversion in the same year, coordinating two strategies that are often planned separately, if they are planned together at all.</p>

<h3>Bracket and Threshold Management</h3>

<p>Because realized losses offset realized gains dollar for dollar, harvesting can help keep your taxable income below key thresholds, such as the top of the 0% or 15% long-term capital gains bracket, or the Net Investment Income Tax threshold discussed below. A gain that would otherwise push you into a higher bracket in a single year can sometimes be absorbed by losses harvested earlier in that same year.</p>

<h3>Charitable Giving and Concentrated Positions</h3>

<p>Direct indexing pairs naturally with charitable giving strategy. Appreciated positions inside the account can be donated directly to a donor-advised fund, avoiding capital gains tax on the appreciation while still generating a charitable deduction, while separate, unrelated positions showing losses are harvested for a tax benefit. For clients working through a concentrated stock position from an executive role or a business sale, our <a href="/blog/tax-efficient-diversification-unwind-concentrated-stock">guide to tax-efficient diversification</a> covers complementary tools, including exchange funds and structured selling plans, that can be coordinated alongside a direct indexing account. Business owners and startup employees managing <a href="/blog/equity-compensation-planning-startup-employees">equity compensation</a> often face a similar version of this same coordination problem, just with a different source of concentration.</p>

<h2>2026 Capital Gains Tax Brackets: Why the Rate You Offset Matters</h2>

<p>The value of a harvested loss depends on the rate of the gain it offsets. Long-term capital gains, on assets held more than one year, are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20%, depending on your total taxable income for the year.</p>

<h2>The Limits of Tax-Loss Harvesting: An Honest Assessment</h2>

<p>A coordinated tax plan is built on a realistic view of what a strategy can and cannot do. Tax-loss harvesting, including at the security level through direct indexing, has real limitations worth understanding before adopting it.</p>

<p><strong>It doesn&rsquo;t help in tax-advantaged accounts.</strong> IRAs, 401(k)s, and Roth accounts do not currently recognize taxable gains or losses, so there is nothing to harvest. Direct indexing&rsquo;s tax benefit is specific to taxable brokerage accounts.</p>

<p><strong>The opportunity front-loads and then fades.</strong> A newly funded direct indexing account typically has the most harvesting opportunity, since every position starts near its purchase price and market movement quickly creates dispersion. As years pass and the market generally appreciates, the embedded cost basis of the underlying positions rises toward current market value, and the pool of available losses to harvest shrinks. A direct indexing account funded during a strong, multi-year bull market may produce meaningfully less harvesting benefit than one funded shortly before a market decline.</p>

<p><strong>There is a cost and tracking-error trade-off.</strong> Owning hundreds of individual securities, rebalancing them, and executing wash-sale-aware trades involves more transaction activity and typically a higher management fee than a passive index fund or ETF. The account&rsquo;s performance can also deviate somewhat from the benchmark index it is designed to track, particularly in smaller accounts or those with significant customization. The tax benefit needs to outweigh that added cost and tracking error for the strategy to be worthwhile, and for smaller accounts or investors in lower tax brackets, it may not.</p>

<p><strong>It is not a substitute for a sound asset allocation.</strong> Tax-loss harvesting is a tax-efficiency overlay, not an investment strategy in its own right, and it should never be the reason to hold an allocation that does not otherwise fit your goals, time horizon, and risk tolerance. If the real issue is an oversized position in a single company, as covered in our guide to <a href="/blog/tax-efficient-diversification-unwind-concentrated-stock">unwinding a concentrated stock position</a>, tax-loss harvesting inside a separate direct indexing account does not solve that underlying concentration problem.</p>

<p><strong>Results are not guaranteed and vary by year.</strong> In a year when a market or index rises steadily with low dispersion among individual stocks, there may be little or nothing to harvest at all. Direct indexing is designed to seek tax efficiency when the opportunity exists; it cannot manufacture losses that the market has not created.</p>
