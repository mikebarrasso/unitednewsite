<p><strong>High-net-worth households often face several planning decisions at once: taxes, investments, equity compensation, retirement income, and legacy. This guide explains where the complexity tends to appear and how coordinating these areas can help decisions work together. Every situation differs, and each approach involves trade-offs.</strong></p>

<h2>What High-Net-Worth Financial Planning Means</h2>

<p>There is no single definition of "high net worth." Banks, research firms, and advisory firms each draw the line differently, and a dollar figure says little about what a household actually needs. In practice, planning becomes more involved when several of these are present at once: multiple income sources, concentrated stock, a business, a large taxable investment account, or a desire to pass assets to family or charity.</p>

<p>The common thread is that decisions in one area tend to affect the others. A stock sale changes the tax return. A Roth conversion changes future required distributions. A gift changes an estate. Planning for these households is less about any single product and more about the order and timing of decisions.</p>

<h2>Coordinating Taxes and Investments</h2>

<p>For households with meaningful taxable accounts, the tax consequences of an investment decision can be as important as the decision itself. Areas worth reviewing include:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Asset location.</strong> Which investments sit in taxable, tax-deferred, or Roth accounts can affect the taxes you pay over time, though the right mix depends on your situation.</li>
<li><strong>Realizing gains and losses.</strong> Losses in a taxable account may offset gains, subject to annual limits and the wash-sale rule. Our guide to <a href="/blog/direct-indexing-tax-loss-harvesting">direct indexing and tax-loss harvesting</a> explains how one approach works and where it has limits.</li>
<li><strong>Investment income surtaxes.</strong> Higher-income households may owe an added tax on investment income. Our <a href="/blog/net-investment-income-tax-2026-year-end-guide">year-end guide to the Net Investment Income Tax</a> walks through how it works.</li>
<li><strong>Charitable giving.</strong> Giving appreciated assets or using certain giving vehicles can change the tax picture, and the rules have conditions worth confirming with your CPA.</li>
</ul>

<p>None of these guarantees a lower tax bill. They are decisions to make deliberately, with the tax return in front of you. That is where <a href="/services/tax-planning">tax planning</a> and <a href="/services/investment-management">investment management</a> benefit from working together.</p>

<h2>Equity Compensation and Concentrated Positions</h2>

<p>Many executives and engineers hold a large share of their wealth in employer stock, options, or restricted stock units. The tax treatment differs by award type, and the timing of an exercise or sale can affect both the current-year return and future flexibility. Holding a large position in one company also concentrates risk, which is separate from the tax question.</p>

<p>For a deeper look, see our guides on <a href="/blog/equity-compensation-isos-vs-nsos-ny-startup-employees">ISOs versus NSOs</a>, <a href="/blog/qsbs-exclusion-section-1202-planning">the QSBS exclusion</a>, and <a href="/blog/tax-efficient-diversification-unwind-concentrated-stock">diversifying a concentrated stock position</a>. Our <a href="/services/equity-compensation">equity compensation service</a> page explains how the team approaches these decisions.</p>

<h2>Estate and Legacy Coordination</h2>

<p>Estate documents are drafted by an attorney, and we do not provide legal advice. What a financial plan can do is make sure the documents and the accounts agree. Common items to review include beneficiary designations on retirement accounts, how assets are titled, whether trusts are funded as intended, and how charitable or family gifts fit your cash-flow needs.</p>

<p>Beneficiary rules can change the tax result for heirs. Our guide to the <a href="/blog/inherited-ira-10-year-rule">inherited IRA 10-year rule</a> shows why these details matter.</p>

<h2>The Years Before Retirement</h2>

<p>The five to ten years before retirement are often when planning decisions carry the most weight, because income is high, account balances are large, and choices about Roth conversions, Social Security, and withdrawals begin to overlap. Our guides on <a href="/blog/pre-retirement-tax-transition-managing-brackets-rmds">managing tax brackets before RMDs</a>, <a href="/blog/roth-conversion-strategy-pre-retirees">the Roth conversion window</a>, and <a href="/blog/social-security-claiming-strategy">Social Security claiming</a> cover these in detail.</p>

<h2>Protecting What You Have Built</h2>

<p>Protection is part of a complete plan, though it is often handled by other professionals. A periodic review can include liability and umbrella coverage, disability and long-term care considerations, and how assets are titled. We coordinate with your attorney and insurance professionals so this review connects to the rest of the plan. Protection tools involve costs and limits, and not every household needs each one.</p>

<h2>An Annual Planning Checklist</h2>

<ul>
<li>Project this year's income and estimated taxes before year-end</li>
<li>Review taxable accounts for gains, losses, and concentrated positions</li>
<li>Check upcoming equity award vesting, exercise, or sale dates</li>
<li>Confirm beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and insurance</li>
<li>Review your estate documents with your attorney after major life changes</li>
<li>Update retirement income and withdrawal assumptions</li>
<li>Confirm insurance coverage still fits your circumstances</li>
</ul>
